Prop 03 — Income tax rates

This measure would keep California’s higher-income tax rates in place beyond 2031.

Open official video on YouTube ↗
  1. 1The proposal+

    Makes permanent the existing voter-approved tax rates on higher income, which are currently scheduled to expire in 2031.

  2. 2What would change?+

    The higher rates would continue past 2031. The official summary also describes allocating the tax revenue to public education. The state estimates this would maintain about $5 billion to $15 billion in annual income tax revenue. See the official analysis for assumptions and detail.

  3. 3Arguments and perspectives+

    Supporters emphasize continued funding for schools and health care. Opponents raise concerns about making a temporary tax increase permanent and how state revenue is spent.

  4. 4Potential impact+

    The main fiscal question is whether current high-income tax revenue continues after 2031, including the measure’s school-funding allocation.

  5. 5Sources and further reading+

    Review the full summary, impartial fiscal analysis, and arguments provided in the official guide.

Prop 04 · Campaign finance

Prop 04 — Public campaign financing

This measure would let state and local governments establish public campaign-financing programs for candidates under specified conditions.

Legislative constitutional amendment
Official quick-reference guide
ASL interpretation, audio, and on-screen textOpen video on YouTube ↗

At a glance

  • Allows public campaign-financing programs under specified conditions
  • Program rules would define eligibility and spending
  • The measure itself does not launch a program
Official analysis and arguments ↗
  1. 1The proposal+

    Repeals the general prohibition on state and local governments using public funds for candidate election campaigns, allowing public financing programs to be created.

  2. 2What would change?+

    Future programs would have to follow rules about eligibility, spending limits, and how candidates may use funds. Money earmarked for education, transportation, or public safety could not be used for these campaigns. The measure permits programs; it does not itself launch one.

  3. 3Arguments and perspectives+

    Supporters point to public financing as another way to structure candidate campaigns. Questions for consideration include program design, eligibility rules, and use of public funds.

    Review official analysis and arguments ↗

  4. 4Potential impact+

    The state estimates ongoing costs of a few hundred thousand dollars each year for the Fair Political Practices Commission to respond to questions about future programs.

  5. 5Sources and further reading+

    The official guide explains the rules and lists the formal arguments submitted by proponents and opponents.

Prop 38 · Immunology research

Prop 38 — Immunology research

This measure would authorize $8.4 billion in state bonds for immunology and immunotherapy research in California.

State general-obligation bond
Official quick-reference guide
ASL interpretation, audio, and on-screen textOpen video on YouTube ↗

At a glance

  • Authorizes $8.4 billion in state bonds
  • Funding is allocated to research and grants
  • Repayment is spread over many years
Official analysis and arguments ↗
  1. 1The proposal+

    Authorizes $8.4 billion in general-obligation bonds to support research into immunology and immunotherapy.

  2. 2What would change?+

    Half of the bond funding would go to one UC-affiliated nonprofit research institute, and half would support research grants.

  3. 3Arguments and perspectives+

    Supporters emphasize additional resources for medical research. Opponents raise concerns about long-term debt and the way funding is allocated.

  4. 4Potential impact+

    The state estimates bond repayment would cost about $500 million to $600 million annually for roughly 20 years. Some or all of that cost could be offset if funded research generates revenue.

  5. 5Sources and further reading+

    Read the fiscal estimate, bond terms, proposed funding structure, and official arguments.

Prop 39 · Voter identification

Prop 39 — Voter identification

This measure would require government-issued identification for in-person voting and additional identification information on mail-ballot envelopes.

Initiative constitutional amendment
Official quick-reference guide
ASL interpretation, audio, and on-screen textOpen video on YouTube ↗

At a glance

  • Creates government-issued ID requirements for in-person voting
  • Adds ID information to mail-ballot envelopes
  • Includes annual county reporting requirements
Official analysis and arguments ↗
  1. 1The proposal+

    Creates new requirements for voters to provide government-issued identification when voting.

  2. 2What would change?+

    For in-person voting, voters would present government-issued identification. For mail ballots, the last four digits of a designated government ID number would need to be written on the envelope. The measure would also require county election officials to report annually the share of voters whose citizenship status they have verified.

  3. 3Arguments and perspectives+

    Supporters say identification requirements can strengthen confidence in election procedures. Opponents raise concerns about implementation costs, personal information, and barriers for eligible voters.

    Review official analysis and arguments ↗

  4. 4Potential impact+

    The state estimates annual implementation costs ranging from tens of millions of dollars to the low hundreds of millions of dollars.

  5. 5Sources and further reading+

    Check the official summary for the exact identification requirements, fiscal estimate, and arguments submitted by both sides.

Prop 43 · Local tax measures

Prop 43 — Local tax measures

This measure would raise the voter-approval threshold from a majority to two-thirds for certain voter-proposed local special taxes, beginning January 1, 2027.

Legislative constitutional amendment
Official quick-reference guide
ASL interpretation, audio, and on-screen textOpen video on YouTube ↗

At a glance

  • Raises the threshold for certain voter-proposed local special taxes
  • Change begins January 1, 2027
  • Could affect future local tax revenues
Official analysis and arguments ↗
  1. 1The proposal+

    Raises the approval threshold for certain local special taxes proposed by voter initiative from a majority to two-thirds.

  2. 2What would change?+

    Starting January 1, 2027, the specified voter-proposed local special taxes would need two-thirds approval instead of more than 50%.

  3. 3Arguments and perspectives+

    Supporters emphasize a higher approval threshold for local tax increases. Opponents argue that it could make it harder for communities to fund local services through special taxes.

    Review official analysis and arguments ↗

  4. 4Potential impact+

    The official fiscal summary says local government tax revenues might not increase as much in the future because of the higher threshold.

  5. 5Sources and further reading+

    Consult the official analysis for which taxes are covered, the timing, and the submitted arguments for and against the measure.
